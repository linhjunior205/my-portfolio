> **Classic Reads** is a series where I summarize influential essays in software engineering and ask what they still teach us today.

## The essay

**Peter Norvig** — co-author of the classic AI textbook *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* and longtime Director of Research at Google — wrote **"Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years"** around **1998** and has updated it since.

He noticed bookstore shelves full of titles like *Teach Yourself Java in 24 Hours* and asked: what can you actually learn in 24 hours? His answer: some syntax — but not how to program well.

## The core argument

Norvig cites research on expertise (including work by Hayes, Bloom and others) showing that in many fields — chess, music, sports, programming — reaching **world-class expertise takes about ten years** of deliberate practice. This is related to the widely quoted "10,000 hours" idea.

The key word is **deliberate**: not just doing the same thing for years, but practicing at the edge of your ability, getting feedback and improving.

## Norvig's recipe

His advice for becoming a good programmer, summarized:

- **Get interested and program because it is fun.** Make sure it stays fun enough that you will put in ten years.
- **Program.** The best kind of learning is learning by doing.
- **Talk with other programmers;** read other people's programs.
- **Work on projects with other programmers.** Be the best on some projects and the worst on others.
- **Work on projects after other programmers.** Understand and maintain a program someone else wrote. Learn what makes code easy to maintain.
- **Learn at least half a dozen languages**, including ones with different ideas: one with class abstractions, one that is functional, one that is declarative, one that supports concurrency.
- **Know how the computer works:** how long it takes to read memory, read from disk, or send a packet across the network.
- **Get involved in a language standardization effort,** or at least understand why languages are designed the way they are.
- **Have the good sense to get off the standardization effort** as quickly as possible.

He also includes a famous table of approximate timings (memory access, disk seek, network round trip) — the origin of the "latency numbers every programmer should know" that engineers still share today.

## Why it hits harder in the AI era

In 2026, you really can get a working app "in 24 hours" — an AI agent will write most of it. So is Norvig's advice outdated?

The opposite. AI makes the **first 24 hours** much shorter, but it does not shorten the path to **judgment**:

- Knowing whether the generated design will survive real users and real data.
- Spotting the subtle bug, the missing index, the security hole.
- Maintaining a system for years as requirements change.

Those come from years of building, breaking, reading and maintaining real programs — exactly what Norvig describes. AI is a fantastic accelerator for deliberate practice (instant explanations, fast experiments), but only if you use it to **understand**, not just to produce.

## A practical plan for juniors

Inspired by Norvig, here is what I try to do:

1. **Build real things** and deploy them, even small ones.
2. **Read good code** — open-source libraries you use every day.
3. **Maintain something** for months, not just start new projects.
4. **Learn a different paradigm** every year or two (functional, systems programming, SQL deeply).
5. **Learn the fundamentals under the abstractions**: networks, databases, operating systems.
6. **Use AI as a tutor**: ask it *why*, then verify.
7. **Write about what you learn** — explaining is the fastest way to find gaps.

## Takeaways

- Real skill takes years of deliberate practice; there are no shortcuts to judgment.
- Variety matters: many languages, many projects, many roles in a team.
- Learn how computers actually work.
- AI shortens the first day, not the ten years — use it to learn faster, not to skip learning.
