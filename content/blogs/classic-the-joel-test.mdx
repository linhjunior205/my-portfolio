> **Classic Reads** is a series where I summarize influential essays in software engineering and ask what they still teach us today.

## The essay

In August 2000, **Joel Spolsky** — co-founder of Fog Creek Software (where Trello was born) and later of Stack Overflow — published **"The Joel Test: 12 Steps to Better Code"** on his blog *Joel on Software*.

The idea was deliberately simple: twelve **yes/no** questions you can answer in minutes. A score of 12 is perfect; Joel wrote that 11 is tolerable, and 10 or lower means serious problems.

## The twelve questions

1. **Do you use source control?**
2. **Can you make a build in one step?**
3. **Do you make daily builds?**
4. **Do you have a bug database?**
5. **Do you fix bugs before writing new code?**
6. **Do you have an up-to-date schedule?**
7. **Do you have a spec?**
8. **Do programmers have quiet working conditions?**
9. **Do you use the best tools money can buy?**
10. **Do you have testers?**
11. **Do new candidates write code during their interview?**
12. **Do you do hallway usability testing?**

## Why it worked

- **Fast and concrete.** No maturity model, no 200-page process document — just questions anyone can answer.
- **Useful for job seekers.** Developers started asking interviewers these questions to judge a company before joining.
- **Each question hides a deeper principle**: reproducibility (1–3), quality first (4–5), planning (6–7), developer productivity (8–9), quality assurance (10), hiring well (11) and user focus (12).

## How it holds up in 2026

Many questions have become so standard that "yes" is the minimum:

- **Source control** – Git is universal.
- **One-step builds and daily builds** – evolved into **continuous integration**: every push is built and tested automatically.
- **Bug database** – issue trackers like GitHub Issues, Jira or Linear are everywhere.

Some remain surprisingly hard:

- **Fix bugs before writing new code** – feature pressure still wins too often.
- **Up-to-date schedule and specs** – replaced by roadmaps, user stories and design docs, but keeping them current is still rare.
- **Quiet working conditions** – open offices, constant chat notifications and back-to-back meetings make focus time a real problem.
- **Hallway usability testing** – showing your UI to a few people for five minutes is still one of the cheapest, most skipped quality practices.

And some have changed meaning:

- **Testers** – many teams no longer have separate QA; developers own automated tests. The principle — someone must look at quality systematically — still stands.
- **Coding in interviews** – still common, but now debated: take-home projects, pair programming sessions and "work with an AI assistant" exercises are alternatives.

## A 2026 version of the test

If I wrote a modern update, I would keep the spirit and add:

1. Does every change go through **code review**?
2. Does **CI run tests** on every pull request?
3. Can you **deploy to production in one step**, and roll back just as easily?
4. Do you have **monitoring and alerts** that tell you about problems before users do?
5. Are **secrets kept out of the code** and rotated?
6. Can a new developer get the project **running locally in under a day**?
7. Is there written **documentation for how the system and the team work**, kept in the repo?
8. Do developers get **uninterrupted focus time** every day?
9. Do you have clear rules for **using AI tools** — what data can be shared and how generated code is reviewed?
10. Do you **talk to real users** regularly?

## Takeaways

- Use the Joel Test (or a modern version) to evaluate teams you join — and the team you are in.
- Simple, concrete checklists beat vague "best practices".
- Most items are about **feedback loops**: build, test, bug, user. Fast feedback is still the heart of good software teams.
