> **Classic Reads** is a series where I summarize influential essays in software engineering and ask what they still teach us today.

## The essay

**Dan McKinley**, an engineer who spent years at Etsy, published **"Choose Boring Technology"** in **2015**, based on a talk he gave. It is short, funny and has saved many teams from expensive mistakes.

## Innovation tokens

The central idea: every company gets **about three innovation tokens**. You can spend them on anything new and unproven — but once they are gone, they are gone.

If your product itself is innovative (a new kind of app, a new market), you have probably already spent a token. Using a brand-new database, a brand-new language **and** a brand-new deployment platform on top of that means you are betting the company on several unknowns at once.

## What "boring" means

Boring does not mean bad. Boring means **well understood**:

- Its **failure modes are known**. When PostgreSQL or MySQL breaks, thousands of people have seen that exact problem and written about it.
- It is **documented**, with answers already on Stack Overflow.
- **People know it**, so hiring and onboarding are easier.
- It has been **in production for years** under real load.

New technology has **unknown unknowns** — problems nobody has discovered yet. You will discover them, in production, at the worst possible time.

## The cost of many technologies

McKinley's second point is about the total number of tools. Every technology you add brings ongoing costs: monitoring, upgrades, security patches, expertise, documentation. Each one is cheap alone; together they create a huge operational surface.

He illustrates it with a story: adding a new tool for one problem looks locally optimal, but the **whole system** is better off solving most problems with the few tools you already run well — even if each is not the theoretically best fit.

## How to add new technology responsibly

The essay does not ban new things. It asks for a process:

1. **Write down the problem** you are trying to solve.
2. **Try to solve it with what you already have.** Ask: "how would we do this with our current stack?"
3. If that is truly painful, **discuss it openly** with the team — new technology affects everyone who operates it.
4. If you adopt it, **commit to it**: replace the old way rather than running both forever.

## Boring technology in 2026

The essay maps directly to today's choices:

- **"Just use Postgres"** for relational data, JSON, search and even queues is the boring-technology argument in action.
- A **modular monolith** before microservices.
- **Mature frameworks** (Next.js, NestJS, Django, Rails, Spring) over this month's new framework for production systems.
- **Managed services** instead of running your own clusters.

And AI tools add a new angle: coding agents are **much better with boring technology**, because there is far more training data and documentation for popular, stable tools. An agent will write better PostgreSQL than code for a database released last month.

## Where to spend your tokens

Spend innovation tokens where they create real advantage **for your users** — usually in your core product, not in your infrastructure. If your product's value is AI-powered reading feedback, spend tokens on the AI, and keep the database, auth and deployment boring.

For personal learning, the rule is different: side projects are the perfect place to play with exciting new tools.

## Takeaways

- You have only a few innovation tokens; spend them on what makes your product special.
- Prefer technologies whose failure modes are well known.
- Minimize the total number of technologies you operate.
- Experiment freely in side projects, not in the core of production systems.
