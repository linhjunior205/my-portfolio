> **Classic Reads** is a series where I summarize influential essays in software engineering and ask what they still teach us today.

## The book

**The Mythical Man-Month: Essays on Software Engineering** was published by **Fred Brooks** in **1975**, based on his experience managing IBM's OS/360 operating system. The 20th-anniversary edition (1995) added "No Silver Bullet" and a retrospective chapter. It is one of the most quoted books in our industry — and, as the joke goes, one of the least read.

## The man-month is a myth

Project plans often treat effort as interchangeable: if a job takes 12 person-months, then 4 people can do it in 3 months, or 12 people in 1 month.

Brooks shows why this fails for software:

- **Tasks cannot be perfectly divided.** Some work is sequential. Nine women cannot make a baby in one month.
- **Communication grows faster than team size.** With *n* people there are *n(n−1)/2* possible communication paths. 5 people: 10 paths. 20 people: 190 paths.
- **New people need training** — usually by the experienced people who were doing the work.

## Brooks's Law

From this comes the most famous line in the book:

> "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."

New members slow everyone down before they speed anything up. Brooks himself called the law an "outrageous oversimplification", but the core insight survives: **you cannot rescue a late project just by adding people.**

## Other ideas worth knowing

### Conceptual integrity

The most important consideration in system design is that the system reflects **one coherent set of design ideas**, ideally from one mind or a small group. A product with fewer features but consistent design beats one with many features stitched together.

### The surgical team

Brooks proposed organizing teams around one chief programmer supported by specialists, like a surgeon with assistants — an early argument for small, focused teams with clear roles.

### The second-system effect

The second system an architect designs tends to be over-engineered, packed with every feature that was cut from the first. Watch out for "this time we'll do it properly" rewrites.

### Plan to throw one away

"Plan to throw one away; you will, anyhow." The first version teaches you what you should have built. (Brooks later softened this in favour of incremental development.)

### No silver bullet for estimates

He also observed that programmers are optimists and that schedules slip "one day at a time" — a reminder to track progress continuously.

## What still holds in 2026

- **Brooks's Law** explains why emergency hiring rarely saves a deadline. Cutting scope or moving the date usually works better.
- **Communication overhead** is why companies favour small autonomous teams ("two-pizza teams") with clear interfaces between them.
- **Conceptual integrity** is why great products often have a strong design owner — and why consistent architecture matters even more when AI agents generate code across the codebase.

## What changed

- Tooling, version control, CI and async communication have reduced (not removed) onboarding and coordination costs.
- Iterative, incremental delivery has replaced big upfront plans.
- AI agents add a new twist: they are "team members" that need context but no training time. They can help a late project — but the humans still need to coordinate, review and integrate their work.

## Takeaways

- When a project is late, prefer **cutting scope** over adding people.
- Keep teams small and interfaces between teams clear.
- Protect the design's conceptual integrity.
- Beware the second-system effect when planning a rewrite.
